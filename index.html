    Why the lunar south pole is hard

    At the pole the Sun never rises more than about 1.6° above the ideal horizon, so it skims around the sky once every 29.5 days. A ridge a few kilometres away can hide it for days. Earth hangs low too, wobbling up and down by about ±7° through libration, so whether a lander can talk straight to Earth depends on the exact spot and date.

    What Crescent does

    1. Real terrain. We cast 720 rays from each site across the NASA LRO LOLA polar elevation model (120 m) out to 440 km, accounting for the Moon's curvature, to get the true horizon in every direction.
    2. Real sky. Hourly sub-solar and sub-Earth points on the Moon (2026–2030) come from NASA JPL Horizons (DE441, MOON_ME frame, the same frame as LOLA). We correct for parallax, since the site sits 1,737 km from the Moon's centre.
    3. Power & comms. Sunlight = the fraction of the solar disk above the terrain. Direct-to-Earth = Earth's centre above the terrain plus your antenna mask.
    4. Decisions. Compare sites, weight what matters to your mission, and find the dates when a stay of a given length gets continuous sunlight and the longest Earth contact.

    Checks

    Our 2027 results reproduce published patterns: the Shackleton–de Gerlache connecting ridge is sunlit about 86% of the year, while Malapert Massif and Mons Mouton trade some sunlight for Earth in view 80–86% of the time.

    Limits

    Featured sites are representative high points near NASA's Artemis III candidate regions, not official landing coordinates. Terrain resolution (120 m for featured sites, 300 m for custom sites) cannot show boulders or small local slopes. This is an educational and early-planning tool, not flight software.
